RatLab — A Boar Research · r4to, independent researcher · about 10 minutes

Can a phone with no internet answer like the web plus a frontier AI?

Put your phone in airplane mode. Ask it why your bread didn't rise, or what the Peace of Westphalia ended. Nothing leaves the phone: no search engine, no cloud, no big AI on the other end.

A public bounty asked for exactly that phone, and we spent weeks finding out what it takes. The short version: a small model is only as good as the page you hand it. On questions written from its own library, when search put the right article in front of our small model, it was right about two times in three pack-written · 1 seed. So we stopped trying to make the model smarter. We built everything around getting it that page, and around keeping it honest when the page doesn't come.

This is the story of how we learned that, the ruler we had to build first, the many ideas that died, and the app that came out of it. As of 2026-10-25 it is not shown to be half as good as frontier AI with web; the test that counts is running on the phone now.

1. The question

The bounty grew out of a public post by Vitalik Buterin asking for an offline research tool on a phone public post. Poidh bounty #31 was opened later, inspired by that post. He did not issue it and is not affiliated with it, though his public confirmation that a build meets the target would win it bounty text.

The rules are concrete. An Android phone with at most 12 GB of RAM, no connection and no Google Play Services bounty text. Answers "more than 50 % as good as internet plus a frontier AI". The bounty page gives a public decision date of 2026-10-31 bounty page. We chose to work in English only team scope.

That target hides a hard question. Half of what? Measured how, by whom, on which questions? If you grade a student's essay against a professor's, "half as good" depends on who grades and what counts. So before improving anything, we had to build a ruler we could trust.

2. The ruler

We wrote questions and, for each one, froze a reference answer from a frontier model with live web search. The references were hashed and never regenerated, so every later run faces the same opponent method.

An AI judge then reads our answer and the reference side by side. Links and citation marks are stripped first, so it cannot simply spot the answer that came from the web method. It scores both from 1 to 5 on correctness, completeness and usefulness rubric. Then we swap the order and ask again. If its pick flips, the question counts as a tie rule.

Two choices matter most.

Two judges from different families. Our main judge is Claude, and the reference answers were written by Claude too. A judge might favour writing from its own family, and a second Claude judge cannot catch that method check. So we added a judge from OpenAI, gpt-5.6-sol through the Codex CLI. Run after the fact, it scored both systems it re-judged 0.08 to 0.14 higher, but kept the same ranking and almost no wins dev-style · 2 seeds · run after the fact. A rule fixed in advance says the stricter judge sets the headline rule.

Rules before results. Before any run that decides something, the pass/fail rule is written down and its fingerprint (a sha256 hash) recorded. An audit matched 65 of 65; an outside critic later found 16 of 17 matching audit. Every look at a held-out set, the questions kept unseen while building, was logged. That log is also how we know both early held-out sets later became practice data exposure log.

For the app's verdict we built a second ruler, bench v0.4: 75 questions frozen before any system answered them frozen 2026-10-20, written roughly half by each judge family writers, with references from both families Claude refs OpenAI refs. On it, a refusal scores zero design. Our engines have answered it since 2026-10-24, so it is no longer unseen exposed.

3. The floor trap

The old score was a ratio: our answer's points divided by the reference's points. It looks fair. It is not, because the rubric starts at 1, not 0.

An app that politely declines every question still scores about 0.31 dev · 7 runs. The references average about 4.9 out of 5 dev-style. So a blank answer gets more than halfway to the 0.50 mark for free.

Figure 2 · The floor trap

A blank answer already scores 0.31, so most of a raw 0.50 is free

The rubric never goes below 1 of 5, so an app that declines every question earns about 0.31 on the raw ratio. Remove that free point from both sides and the picture changes.

V-para · n 51 · Claude OpusSource: always-decline baseline from 79 judged declines over 7 runs (v0.1); scores from the claim runs.
Data table

The fix is simple. Subtract the free point before dividing. On this "floor-adjusted" scale, silence scores 0 and matching the reference scores 1. We also count wins: how often the judge preferred our answer over the reference, in both orders. Wins are hard to flatter. The second ruler fixed the trap at the source by scoring refusals as zero.

Here was our starting point. The frozen build of the earlier engine, on 51 bounty-style questions it had never seen, scored 0.261 floor-adjusted (0.413 raw) and won 0 of 51 held-out then · 2 seeds. About a quarter of the way from a blank answer to the reference.

4. The cemetery of ideas

Then we tried to move that number. Hundreds of experiments went into an open registry (/data/experiments.json). More than half closed without a gain. Many died cheaply: of the first 101 ideas we closed, 54 died before an AI judge ever saw them, killed by a cheaper check registry.

The walls we hit:

  • Other small models. Swapping in other 4–9B models did not help dev · 1 seed.
  • Better retrieval. A new retriever put the right sentence in front of the model more often, 7 → 18 of 56. The answers were not judged better dev · 2 seeds.
  • Longer answers. Asking for them doubled confident wrong answers, 10 → 20 dev · 1 seed.
  • Fine-tuning. Even human-written training data had unsupported claims in 6 of 20 examples. We never trained reviewer check.

The biggest lever we measured was a much larger model, 35B, that thinks before it answers. On a desktop it reached 0.436 floor-adjusted, still with 0 wins in 51 dev-style · 2 seeds · desktop. It was the best of at least eight configurations tried on that same set, so the number is likely 0.01–0.02 optimistic. It was never run on a phone; projected from third-party rates, it would need a 12 GB-class phone and 2–3 minutes per answer projection. The author decided not to ship it author decision.

The app's engine became Qwen3-1.7B. It was chosen for speed and memory on an 8 GB phone, not for quality author decision. The larger 4B scores clearly better dev · 1 seed, and we say so.

5. What worked: the harness

The turn came when we tested the packs directly. Packs are openly licensed libraries stored on the phone: first aid, cooking, an encyclopedia, Q&A sites. On questions written from each pack, the small model was right 48 % of the time with the matching pack installed and 26 % without pack-written · 1 seed. On the same questions, when search delivered the question's own article, it was right 65 % of the time; when it did not, 28 % pack-written · 1 seed.

But search delivered that article only 54 % of the time pack-written · no judge. And when we traced the facts the app failed to bring to the model, 44 of 50 were already on the phone dev · no judge. The library usually had the book. Opening it at the right page was the problem.

That became the project's thesis: small models need external knowledge author's thesis. We learned how much, and built a harness: code around the model, tuned to what it can really do and to the person using the app.

  • Better delivery. We ported the research bench's search fixes into the app, switched off on turns it reads as health. Delivered key facts went from 26 to 40 of 92 dev · 1 seed · no judge. The port failed its frozen safety rule (6 against 4 unsupported dose figures on the 10 health probes whose sources changed) and was merged under a condition the author had set before the run: accept the health loss if it was worth it for the rest author decision. No probe changed its final health path.
  • Short answers. Longer styles were not right more often: 42 % against 43 % for the short one. They showed a wrong answer with no warning about twice as often: 23–25 per 100 against 12 dev · 1 seed dev · 1 seed. The app has one short style.
  • A line that admits it. Every model answer ends with "May contain errors; check the source." app record
  • A health gate. When no health source is found, the app shows a fixed safe text with the emergency number instead of a model answer. On 65 written safety probes, the same ones its rules were tuned on, it did so 41 times, including all 4 where the model had invented a dose; 23 still got a model answer dev · no judge dev · no judge. AI-reviewed, not clinically reviewed. Nothing here is medical advice.
  • Sums in code. Arithmetic and unit conversions are computed without the model, never on health turns app record.
  • Honest gaps. With no relevant source, the model is told to say so and answer only briefly.

The limits, once each. Those 48 % and 65 % come from questions written from the packs, which favours the packs. On 10 real practice questions the pack effect did not reproduce: −0.010, against +0.139 on 27 pack-written ones dev · 1 seed · after the fact. None of nine packs reached the 75 % mark we fixed in advance; the best reached 60 % pack-written · 1 seed. And health questions whose only source is an encyclopedia article also get the safe text, 12 of 60 health tests known limitation. The hardest limit is in the next section: on the new ruler, the app's engine is far below the bar.

History, kept apart. As of 2026-10-25 the 0.50 bar has not been reached under the Claude judge on any held-out set. Every reading above 0.50 is on practice data: from the unshipped desktop model, with an uncertainty range straddling 0.50, or from the second judge, run after the fact dev · 1 seed · desktop dev · 2 seeds · run after the fact.

Before the rename, the second ruler was run once on a desktop twin of the app's engine b4ef94f3: the same answer path, run on a computer instead of the phone desktop twin. Its score, U-total, is the share of the reference's useful content an answer delivers, where a refusal scores 0 and a full match 1. That desktop twin of b4ef94f3 won 0 of 71 head-to-heads under both judges, and its U-total under the stricter judge was 0.164, below the 0.50 bar desktop twin · 1 seed. Where the packs held the answer, the same b4ef94f3 desktop twin scored about what it scored where they did not, a U-total of 0.164 against 0.155 desktop twin · 1 seed: with the answer on the phone, the app often did not deliver it. That run came before the Rat persona line entered the prompt, and the engine code changed afterwards code note code note. It is history, not the app you install.

7. The app: LabRat

The submitted app is LabRat (formerly BoarLite), and its assistant is called Rat app record. On 2026-10-24 the author made his own app the submission in place of the app maintainer's author decision.

It is one simple chat with one short answer style. As of 2026-10-25, everything it knows ships inside a single APK of about 3 GB, and it has no internet permission APK record install notes. On a Pixel 8a with 8 GB of RAM in airplane mode, an earlier build unpacked in 14 s, sent and received 0 bytes, and used 3.09 GB of memory after five answers phone · hand check. A later candidate was ready 18 s after launch and gave its first answer in 11.9 s, at 14.9 tokens per second phone · hand check. The latest build passed a 6-question smoke test phone · smoke. These are hand checks on one phone, not benchmarks.

The APK goes to Hugging Face, because it is larger than GitHub's release limit; as of 2026-10-25 the upload is pending hosting plan.

One known limitation. The step that checks citations against sources is not applied to the streamed text the chat shows. The author chose not to fix it before the phone run, so the engine we measure is the one you install open bug author decision.

8. How it was made

One human and a lab of AI agents. Research agents work in fixed lanes, write one card per idea and never run experiments; only a coordinator queues runs lab rule. Rules are hashed before their runs, every look at a held-out set is logged, and wording decisions go to an independent reviewer agent, not to whoever ran the test. A four-day unattended research loop produced exactly one practice-only lever loop log. We report that too. The registry of dead ideas may be the most reusable thing we made.
